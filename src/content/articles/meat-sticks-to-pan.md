It'll let you know.

You've got a piece of chicken breast, a steak, a fillet of fish. It goes in the pan, and thirty seconds later you want to see what's happening underneath. So you poke it. It's stuck. You poke harder. Now there's a strip of your dinner on the pan and a pale, torn piece on your spatula.

The meat was telling you something. You didn't listen.

## Start with the right pan — and a drop of water

Stainless steel, or any pan without coating, is the best for this job. Searing needs real heat, and coated pans aren't built for it.

Preheat it properly. Properly means: flick a drop of water in and it should dance around the pan — a tiny layer of steam keeps it floating. Not hot enough and it just sits there and boils.

Then get that drop out. Even a small one. If you add oil on top of it, it spits, and that becomes dangerous. I've seen it happen. Play the game, then discard the water.

And dry the meat. Pat it dry really well. Otherwise you're killing the heat with the waters you add together with your protein — the pan was hot, and you just put a wet towel on it.

Now the oil — and only now. A high smoking point oil, and more than a drop. A few drops on a stainless steel pan and expecting it not to stick? That's not oil, that's a wish.

## In — and away from you

Lay the meat in from your side to the opposite side, so nothing spills towards your face.

Then leave it.

If you think, oh, I haven't placed it nicely — leave it there. Unless you placed it on the counter. Then yes, you were quite off the target. Otherwise it's fine. Let it do its thing.

I say this before the meat goes in, every time. Because the urge arrives about ten seconds later, and by then it's too late to hear it.

## Read the pan, don't touch the meat

"If it sizzles nicely it's doing its job properly. If too much, then you can lower the heat slightly. If too low, then you start seeing bubbles and waters on the sides of the meat — in that case simply increase the heat."

Waters around your protein is the worst one. Same story as the [soggy vegetables](/soggy-stir-fry-vegetables): not enough heat, and now it's boiling instead of browning.

Then watch the sides. They change colour — whiter for chicken, pork and fish; orange for shrimp; paler than red for beef. Once the sides have turned and the edge looks brown enough, you're allowed to peek. Gently, with tongs or a spatula, from a corner.

"If it's stuck and doesn't let you lift it, just let it be until it lets you lift it."

It sticks? Not ready. Try another corner if you're suspicious it's getting too brown. Otherwise, wait. When the crust is there, it lets go on its own. You don't flip the meat. The meat lets you flip it.

Most people I teach have no patience for this. So I give them a different task — and remind them anyway.

## Minced meat: same rule, different shape

Hot pan, oil in, mince in, spread it out, leave it. You just don't want a ball in your pan. If you start stirring straight away, the heat drops, it goes watery, and you'll never see colour. Let it brown on one side first. Then flip, break it up, give the other pieces a turn on the pan. Then, and only then, stir it properly.

## If you already lifted it

"Simply let it sit again there, make sure it's not burning, and give it slightly more time to get the crust properly. The shape will not be perfect but that's OK. You remember for next time."

One thing this article is not: a chicken thigh with the skin on. That's fatty, it can start in a cold pan, and it won't stick — it'll be even better, because of the fat rendering. Different story. Different article.

And when it's actually cooked inside? Also a different article.

Patience. It's the best thing you can do.
