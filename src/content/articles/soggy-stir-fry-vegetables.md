You're afraid of the wrong thing.

Your pan is on medium. You cut the vegetables nicely, you've got them in, and — nothing. No sound. So you stir. You stir some more.

Five minutes later you're looking at a pan of pale, soft vegetables, sweating in their own water. You didn't want this. Nobody wants this.

I know why the heat was on medium. "But I am afraid of the heat." I hear it every week — four years of teaching hobby cooks, same sentence.

Wrong fear.

## The heat is not what ruins dinner

"You should be afraid of no heat — your veggies will turn mushy. They release waters which cannot evaporate quickly because of the lower heat, and in the end they're cooking in their own juices, which makes them like boiled or steamed. Whereas your intention was not that."

Boiled or steamed — that's a different article.

And the stirring? Every second the food moves, the pan loses the heat it just built. You're cooling it down with your own spoon.

## Turn it up. Then do nothing.

Stir-fry: high. Sauté: medium-high is enough, you don't need the whole dial.

Vegetables in. Hands off. Wait for the sizzle. Then you're good to go.

What you want to hear: sizzle the second the veg touches the pan. What you want to see: browning that takes more than a couple of seconds — not instant black. What you want to eat: vegetables that are still crisp.

## Carrots are a different animal

High heat and a long-cooking vegetable — carrots, broccoli, cauliflower, pumpkin — don't fit in one step. So do a combi.

Sauté on high first, until you get a nice browning and that quick char and flavour. Then add liquid — broth or simple water — and finish them like that. Browning on the outside, cooked on the inside.

How much liquid? Depends on the lid. Lid on: a splash, it won't evaporate fast. No lid: a bit more — a third of the way up the vegetables, up to half for carrots (not broccoli, broccoli is quite thick) — and it's gone by the time they're ready. Want a dry result? Don't drown them.

Ready? Fork test. Pinch a piece: if it falls back into the pan quickly, or slides off when you push it with your finger or a utensil, done. If it sticks on the fork and does nothing — still too hard.

The fork test can also end in your mouth. Most reliable test there is. Just be aware of the heat.

## Your stove is not an excuse

Induction and gas answer fast. Old electric is slower, so you don't have many options: preheat it really well, go high, start. Too hot? Take the pan off the heat for a bit, or add more ingredients — every addition drops the pan temperature for you. Only a few ingredients? Just start. It finishes quickly. No need to play with the heat at all.

## If you overshoot

Browning in 2 seconds and black in 5 — too much. Off the heat for a couple of seconds, stir, next ingredient in. Or turn it down slightly, or simply keep adding.

The heat was never the enemy. Not using it was.
